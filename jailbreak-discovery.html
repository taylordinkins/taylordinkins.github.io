Finding jailbreak attempts with a small labeling budget

Ongoing research · preliminary results · October 2026

A recurring issue in my Ph.D. work on interactive anomaly detection was that statistical outliers don't always match what an expert actually cares about. This project looks at a setting where that mismatch matters a lot: finding jailbreak attempts in LLM traffic when an analyst only has time to review a few dozen prompts.

Novelty isn't malice

The natural way to catch new attacks is to flag whatever looks unusual and have an analyst check it. That assumes attacks are the unusual prompts. In realistic traffic they mostly aren't.

When I looked at the 50 prompts a standard novelty detector ranks as most unusual, about 45 were ordinary chat: requests where the user forgot to paste in the text they wanted edited, pasted code and error logs, and the same template sent over and over. None were attacks. The attacks didn't stand out at all: the typical one ranked near the middle of the benign prompts, and at most 4% of any kind of attack made it into the top 5% a detector would flag.

So a detector that flags whatever looks rare will spend its labels on prompts like these, and with a small budget it may never show the analyst an attack at all. The question I'm working on is how to choose which prompts to label, so that a detector reading the model's internal activations learns what attacks look like rather than what rare prompts look like.

Setup

I hide a small fraction of jailbreak attempts (2% in the results below) in pools of thousands of mostly ordinary prompts, and give each method the same budget of 50 labels from a simulated analyst. Each detector reads an open-weight LLM's internal activations for every prompt, before the model responds. I then score it on a separate held-out set that includes attack types and benign topics it never saw during labeling.

The approach I'm testing is deliberately simple: a small detector that learns from every label, plus a rule for which prompt to show the analyst next. Neither piece is new on its own; the labeling rule builds on established ideas from active learning. What I'm after is when this works, why novelty-based methods fail where it succeeds, and how far it holds up.

Results so far

The first thing to check is whether a method puts attacks in front of the analyst at all. The novelty-based detectors, including PMA from my own prior work, found zero attacks in every run. They spent the whole budget on unusual benign prompts, which is exactly the failure shown above. My approach found about twice as many attacks as active anomaly discovery, the strongest baseline here.

Those labels are what the detector learns from, so finding attacks early carries over to the held-out set. After 50 labels, my approach reaches an AUPRC of 0.40, against 0.23–0.24 for active anomaly discovery and for a detector trained on randomly chosen labels, and 0.14 for a recent detector built on sparse-autoencoder features.

Caveats

The gain isn't uniform. On one of the three pools, random labeling and active anomaly discovery both beat my approach. About one run in six still finds no attack in its 50 labels, and at very low attack rates (1 in 200 prompts) discovery falls apart. The failure of novelty is also specific to realistic traffic: in pools without everyday chat, PMA does well and even comes out ahead of my approach.

False alarms cut both ways. On new benign topics my approach flags about 4% of prompts, compared with about 10% for the distance-based detectors, but it flags about 7% of benign prompts that are phrased like attacks, where those detectors flag almost none. The detector partly learns “sounds adversarial,” not just “is harmful.”

These numbers also come from a setting where the defender starts with no labeled examples. A detector built from public labeled data is a strong starting point on its own, so I'm also testing whether analyst feedback still adds on top of one.

What's next

I'm now running a much broader benchmark ahead of a workshop paper. Compared with the results above, it uses ten fresh data pools and adds:

  • attacks that look nothing like the ones above, including plain harmful requests with no jailbreak wrapping, automatically optimized attacks, and real users' jailbreak attempts;
  • benign topics and later months of traffic held out, to test whether new benign conversations get flagged as threats;
  • attack rates from 0.5% to 5%, and a check of whether the model actually answered each attack it was sent;
  • a second model family, to make sure this isn't a quirk of one model.

The key comparisons and how they'll be judged are written down before the results come in, so the paper can't quietly pick whichever comparison looks best.
