Finding jailbreak attempts with a small labeling budget

Ongoing research · preliminary results · October 2026

A recurring issue in my Ph.D. work on interactive anomaly detection was that statistical outliers don't always match what an expert actually cares about. This project looks at a setting where that mismatch matters a lot: finding jailbreak attempts in LLM traffic when an analyst only has time to review a few dozen prompts.

Disclaimer: Generative AI (Claude) was used to assist the collation and presentation of the results here.

Novelty isn't always malice

A common way to identify anomalies interactively is to flag the most unusual looking instance based on some scoring measure and have an analyst check it. Here, that assumes attacks are the most unusual prompts. In my benchmarks for realistic traffic, it looks like they mostly aren't.

When I looked at 50 prompts a standard unsupervised novelty detector ranked as most “unusual”, about 45 were ordinary chat: requests where the user forgot to paste in the text they wanted edited, pasted code and error logs, and the same template sent over and over. None of these were attacks. The attacks didn't stand out at all: the typical one ranked near the middle of the benign prompts on the score, and at most 4% of any kind of attack made it into the top 5% this detector would flag.

A detector that flags whatever looks rare will spend its labels on prompts like these, and with a small budget it may never show the analyst an attack at all. Often, the first anomaly (or attack) found is needed to inform the detector generally what (some) types of anomalies look like. So, part of what I'm working on is how to choose which prompts to label, so that a detector reading the LLM's internal activations learns what attacks look like rather than what rare prompts look like, and what form the detector should take to incorporate the labeled feedback.

Setup

I hide a small fraction of jailbreak attempts (2% in the results below) in pools of thousands of mostly ordinary prompts, and give each method the same budget of 50 labels from a simulated analyst. Each detector reads an open-weight LLM's internal activations for every prompt, before the model responds. I then score it on a separate held-out set that includes attack types and benign topics it never saw during labeling.

The approach I'm testing is designed to be simple: a small detector that learns from every label, plus a rule for which prompt to show the analyst next. Neither piece is new on its own. The query rule builds on established ideas from active learning, which some existing work has suggested strictly greedy queries are best under a limited budget. What I'm investigating is specific to LLM's: when this works, why existing methods (including my work PMA) fail where this small detector succeeds, and how far it holds up on varied benchmarks.

Results so far

The first thing to check is whether a method puts attacks in front of the analyst at all. The novelty-based detectors, including PMA from my own prior work, found zero attacks in every run. They spent the whole budget on unusual benign prompts, which is exactly the failure shown above. This simple, small detector approach found about twice as many attacks as active anomaly discovery, the strongest baseline here.

Those labels are what the detector learns from, so finding attacks early usually carries over to the held-out set. After 50 labels, my approach reaches an AUPRC of 0.40, against 0.23–0.24 for active anomaly discovery and for a detector trained on randomly chosen labels, and 0.14 for a recent detector built on sparse-autoencoder features.

Caveats

The gain isn't uniform. On one of the three pools, random labeling and active anomaly discovery both beat my approach. About one run in six still finds no attack in its 50 labels, and at very low attack rates (1 in 200 prompts) discovery falls apart. The failure of novelty is also specific to realistic traffic: in pools without everyday chat, PMA does well and even comes out ahead of this approach.

False alarms are inconclusive. On new benign topics my approach flags about 4% of prompts, compared with about 10% for the distance-based detectors, but it flags about 7% of benign prompts that are phrased like attacks, where those detectors flag almost none. The detector partly learns “sounds adversarial,” not just “is harmful.”

These numbers also come from a setting where the defender starts with no labeled examples. A detector built from public labeled data is a strong starting point on its own, so I'm also testing whether analyst feedback still benefits when used on top of one.

In progress

I'm now running a much broader benchmark. It uses ten additional data pools and adds:

  • attacks that look nothing like the ones above, including plain harmful requests with no jailbreak wrapping, automatically optimized attacks, and real users' jailbreak attempts;
  • benign topics and later months of traffic held out, to test whether new benign conversations get flagged as threats;
  • attack rates in the range from 0.5% to 5%, and a check of whether the model actually responded to each attack it was sent;
  • a second model family

Lastly, PMA has some failure cases we tried to address in our paper with PMA-MGE, our version that used a mixture of Gaussians to try to work better on multi-modal data. Initially, this doesn't seem sufficient to handle LLM representations so far. Fully-supervised linear probes seem to perform fairly well on class separation for attacks vs benign prompts in existing literature, so it isn't necessarily a representation issue, but rather how PMA defines anomalies and how it incorporates nominal points. I'm looking into a redesign specific to this use-case that will be informed by these future findings.
